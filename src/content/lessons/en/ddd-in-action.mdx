title: DDD in action
description: ''
problem: >-
  Every service is arranged its own way: its own directory layout, its own words
  for the same things, its own answer to a problem already solved twice next
  door. What you know about one service does not carry to the next — not for the
  author, not at review, not for whoever arrives a year later.
tags: [ddd]
order: 4
deck: ddd-in-action
draft: true
terms:
  DDD: >-
    Domain-Driven Design. A way to describe a service in the words of its
    domain, and to agree on what each of its levels decides.
  ubiquitous language: >-
    One set of words for the whole context: a word has one meaning, and the
    code, the events, the API and the people all spell it the same way.
  bounded context: >-
    The stretch of a system inside which one word has one meaning. Outside it
    the same word may mean something else, and the translation happens at the
    border.
  aggregate: >-
    A piece of state that changes as a whole and by its own rules. The
    transaction boundary: nothing outside can reach past it.
  use case: >-
    One reason the service does something: read, check, change, announce. The
    order of the steps and nothing beyond it.
  adapter: >-
    The translation between the service and what is outside it: a database, a
    queue, somebody else's API. No rules live here, only translation.
  transport: >-
    The edge of the service: how a request arrives and which code answers it.
    No decision about the domain is made here.
steps:
  - id: boundary
    title: What a bounded context is
    narration: >-
      The three departments from the example are three contexts, and each has
      its own truth about the word "payment". Arguing which one is correct goes
      nowhere: all three are, in different places. A bounded context is such a
      place: the stretch of a system inside which a word has one meaning, one
      model, and one team answering for both. The boundary follows the language
      rather than the layers of code or the tables: where a word starts to mean
      something else, the context has ended. What comes next on the diagram are
      the areas of an ordinary shop, and every one of them is such a place.
  - id: estate
    title: What an online shop is made of
    narration: >-
      The catalogue with its basket and checkout. Billing: what the customer
      owes and what has been paid. Delivery: parcels, carriers and where the
      order got to. Customers: who they are and how to reach them. Four areas,
      four teams, four glossaries. There are no arrows yet — who calls whom
      comes later; what matters here is that there is more than one area and
      each knows its own things.
  - id: kinds
    title: Core, supporting, generic
    narration: >-
      Three kinds of context, by what it is to the business. **Core** is what
      makes this shop different from the one next door: the catalogue, the
      prices, working out what the customer owes. That is built in-house and
      staffed with the best people. **Supporting** is needed but wins nothing:
      delivery, the customer record. **Generic** is a solved problem, the same
      everywhere: authentication. Writing your own login gives the shop nothing
      somebody else's would not, so it is bought. The distinction is practical:
      it decides where the money and the people go.
  - id: language
    title: One word, three meanings
    narration: >-
      From here the lesson looks at one context — billing, the most talkative of
      the four. With the boundary drawn, a word inside it can have one meaning.
      Say "payment" outside and everybody hears their own: the product hears the
      checkmark, processing hears the hold, finance hears the statement two days
      later. Everybody nods in the meeting and keeps their own meaning, and the
      disagreement surfaces in the code: one service's `payment.completed` is
      about the checkmark, another's is about money in the account. So the first
      file in the directory is a glossary.
  - id: glossary
    title: What is inside the glossary
    narration: >-
      One line per word: the word and a single definition — the short sentence
      the team is willing to defend. Synonyms have no place here: two words with
      one meaning mean an argument that is still going. The table about the
      three departments comes first: it picks no winner, it says what each of
      the three meanings is called here. The glossary is a file beside the code,
      edited in the same commit; a wiki lives its own life and six months later
      disagrees with what the service does. The rule is simple: a word that is
      not here is not in the code either. Name it first, write it after.

>
**<span data-term="DDD">DDD</span>** answers that with a single agreement: a
service has levels, and each level decides exactly one thing. The
<span data-term="bounded context">bounded context</span> — what the service
owns and which words it uses for it. The
<span data-term="aggregate">aggregate</span> — which states are possible and
which transition between them is legal. The
<span data-term="use case">use case</span> — in what order that happens and who
it calls to do it. The <span data-term="adapter">adapter</span> — how the same
thing sits in a database and travels over the network. The
<span data-term="transport">transport</span> — which code to answer with. The
agreement is the same for every service, so the next service reads like the
last one, and a known problem gets its known answer: it is immediately clear
which level it lives on and where to put it.
</Levels>
