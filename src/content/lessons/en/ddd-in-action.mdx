  for the same things, its own answer to a problem already solved twice next
  door. What you know about one service does not carry to the next — not for the
  author, not at review, not for whoever arrives a year later.

terms:
  DDD: >-
    Domain-Driven Design. A way to describe a service in the words of its
    domain, and to agree on what each of its levels decides.
  ubiquitous language: >-
    One set of words for the whole context: a word has one meaning, and the
    code, the events, the API and the people all spell it the same way.
  bounded context: >-
    The stretch of a system inside which one word has one meaning. Outside it
    the same word may mean something else, and the translation happens at the
    border.
  DTO: >-
    A structure of plain fields in which data crosses a boundary: a request
    body, a table row, a message on the bus. It has no behaviour.
  aggregate: >-
    A cluster of objects that changes as one whole, with a root through which
    everything outside refers to it. The consistency and transaction boundary.
  invariant: >-
    A statement about state that must be true before and after every change.
    Not a check at the door but a property the aggregate keeps at all times.
  application: >-
    The level that defines the jobs the software is supposed to do. It hands
    the work to the domain, holds no domain rules and is kept thin.
  transport: >-
    The point at which the service is called: by a person or by another
    system. No decision about the domain is made here.
steps:
  - id: boundary
    title: What a bounded context is
    narration: |-
      The three departments from the example are three contexts, and each has
      its own truth about the word "payment". Arguing which one is correct goes
      nowhere: all three are, in different places.

      A bounded context is such a place: the stretch of a system inside which a
      word has one meaning, one model, and one team answering for both.

      The language sets the boundary. Where a word starts to mean something
      else, the context has ended; the directory layout and the database schema
      have nothing to do with that boundary — they are chosen inside it.
  - id: estate
    title: What an online shop is made of
    narration: |-
      A customer walks into the shop, and inside the shop there are three areas.

      - **Catalogue** — the basket and the checkout.
      - **Billing** — what the customer owes and what has been paid.
      - **Delivery** — parcels, carriers and where the order got to.

      Three areas, three teams, three glossaries. There is one arrow so far and
      it points at the shop as a whole: who talks to whom inside comes later.
  - id: kinds
    title: Core, supporting, generic
    narration: |-
      The same three areas, now labelled by what each is to the business.

      - **Core** is what makes this shop different from the one next door: the
        catalogue and billing. Built in-house and staffed with the best people.
      - **Supporting** is needed but wins nothing: delivery.
      - **Generic** is a solved problem, the same everywhere: authentication.
        The customer logs in there first and only then walks into the shop, and
        on the diagram it stands outside the boundary: nobody builds it — it is
        bought. Writing your own login gives the shop nothing somebody else's
        would not.

      The distinction is practical: it decides where the money and the people go.
  - id: language
    title: One word, three meanings
    narration: |-
      The customer pays, and the arrow no longer points at the shop as a whole
      but at billing: billing is what owns the word "payment".

      Until the boundary was drawn, the "payment" from the example meant three
      different events, and everybody nodded in the meeting while keeping their
      own; in code that shows up like this: one service's `payment.completed` is
      about the checkmark, another's is about money in the account.

      So the first file in billing's directory is a glossary.
  - id: glossary
    title: What is inside the glossary
    narration: |-
      One line per word: the word and a single definition — the short sentence
      the team is willing to defend. Synonyms have no place here: two words with
      one meaning mean an argument that is still going.

      The table about the three departments comes first: it picks no winner, it
      says what each of the three meanings is called here.

      The glossary is a file beside the code, edited in the same commit; a wiki
      lives its own life and six months later disagrees with what the service
      does. The rule is simple: a word that is not here is not in the code
      either. Name it first, write it after.

>
**<span data-term="DDD">DDD</span>** answers that with a single agreement: a
service has levels, and each level decides exactly one thing. Knowledge only
points down: a level knows the one below it and nothing about the one above.
That is why the domain depends on neither the database nor the protocol it was
called over.
</Levels>

What follows is a walkthrough of one example. An online shop: the catalogue,
delivery, customers and billing.

Say "payment" inside it and every department hears its own thing.

- **Product.** The purchase went through, the customer saw the checkmark.
- **Processing.** The money is held, nothing was captured.
- **Finance.** The money will show up in a statement two days later, or will
  not show up at all.

Three different events under one word — and that is where the walkthrough
starts.
