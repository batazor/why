title: 'System design: panda clips'
description: >-
  An interview task from the brief to the diagram: upload a video, find the
  panda, cut the clips, let people tag, search and watch them. Why there is no
  broker here, and why every decision is about bytes.
problem: 'A video task gets solved as a queue task: Kafka, workers, arrows. But the flow here is a fraction of a video per second, and the queue is not the problem. The problem is that one video weighs hundreds of megabytes and a minute of GPU, and until that is counted nobody knows where the bytes should go, what to keep, or where to serve from.'

  text: >-
    Design a service that lets users extract and store panda clips from
    uploaded videos. Users can tag clips with words so that other users can
    search for and watch those clips. The analogy: you go to a zoo, record a
    video, upload it to the system, and the system automatically cuts out the
    parts where a panda is in the frame. Those clips can then be tagged,
    watched, shared and searched, like on YouTube.

    title: What is actually being asked
    narration: |-
      You retell the task in your own words and check it with whoever brought
      it. The paragraph above has no numbers, but it has four promises, and
      each has a price.

      - **Accept a video and release.** The user uploads and leaves. Clips come
        later: between upload and result there is processing that takes longer
        than anyone will hold a connection open.
      - **Find the panda on its own.** Nobody labels videos by hand. So there is
        a neural [[detector]] inside, and it has confidence, errors and a GPU
        price.
      - **Store the clips and let people watch them.** A clip is a file that
        sits somewhere for a long time and is watched many times. That is
        about bytes, not about states.
      - **Tag and search.** People add words, other people search by words. We
        need a tag dictionary and a search that sees a fresh tag.

      The interviewer's YouTube analogy sets the proportion: few upload, many
      watch. Everything asked fits in one word, video, and that word decides
      what the system will be: a clip weighs hundreds of megabytes, while a
      request to the scraping service weighed kilobytes.

      What the brief lacks: how many videos, how long, from what device, how
      soon the clips are needed, and whether the original is needed after
      cutting. That is the next step.
  - id: questions
    title: The questions without which there is nothing to calculate
    narration: |-
      This wording fits both one machine with a graphics card and a GPU farm in
      three regions. Which one we need depends on answers the brief does not
      contain.

        day and five thousand hour-long ones are tasks an order of magnitude
        apart in GPU and in disk. Without this number no estimate adds up.
      - **What are they shot with?** A phone records 1080p at about 10 Mbit/s:

      - **Is the original needed after cutting?** If not, storage shrinks by an

      - **How soon are the clips needed?** Minutes: a normal pool. Seconds:

        own state and a message to the user.
      - **What counts as a clip?** Minimum length, padding at the edges, the
        pause after which the panda "left the frame". These are detector
        rules, and product sets them.
      - **Who watches, and from where?** Public and worldwide means a CDN
        serves the bytes, not our server.
      - **Are tags free words or a dictionary? Who adds them?** This decides
        search and whether moderation is needed.

      Answers in words go into requirements, numbers go into estimates. Where
      product says "don't know", we write down an assumption and check it
      with a number.
  - id: actors
    title: Who uses the system
    narration: |-

      differs from the viewer's not only in endpoints but in bytes: one pours
      in hundreds of megabytes, the others pull megabytes out, and neither
      should pass through our API.

  - id: nfr
    title: Non-functional requirements
    narration: |-
      The second section is properties. A non-functional requirement adds no
      actions to the system; it sets a guarantee for the ones that exist.

      They are checked through consequences, so under each one it says what
      breaks without it. This task has three properties the scraper did not
      have, and all three are about bytes.

  - id: slo
    title: The numbers we commit to
    narration: |-
      A property without a number cannot be checked. An [[SLO]] sets the
      share, the limit and the window.

      - **Upload intake: 99.9% over 30 days.** Start and complete answer even
        when the GPU pool is down. That is 43 minutes a month when uploading is
        impossible.
      - **99% of videos reach a final state within a day with no human.** The
        final states are ready, no pandas, and failed. A video stuck in
        processing forever is worse than a failed one.
      - **95% of videos up to ten minutes get their clips within 15 minutes.**
        This is a promise about the queue and the pool. It breaks when uploads
        outpace what the GPUs work off.
      - **The first frame within two seconds in 95 cases out of 100, from any
        country.** We count the upper bound, not the average, and not only
        from our own city.
      - **Search within 300 ms in 99 cases out of 100, and a fresh tag visible
        within five seconds.** The second number matters more: a search that
        cannot find a tag added a minute ago looks broken.

      The targets go into the column next to the rows already in the table.
      Decisions are made by these numbers later: the third one broken, we
      grow the pool; the fourth one broken, we look at the CDN and the segment
      length.
  - id: calc-pipeline
    title: 'Estimate: how many GPUs'
    narration: |-
      The calculator is on the right. Five thousand videos a day is 0.06
      videos a second on average and 0.23 at peak, if the peak is four times
      the average. For comparison, the
      [scraping jobs service](../scraping-jobs/) had 28 jobs a second. The
      flow here is a hundred times smaller, and at this flow the queue is not a
      question. The question is what happens to each video.

      Each video costs GPU time. Not every frame: the model looks at two frames
      a second, and a five-minute video takes about a minute, 0.2 seconds of
      GPU per second of video. That is 83 GPU hours a day.

      How many GPUs to keep is given by [[Little's law]]: the flow at peak
      multiplied by the time of one processing run. 0.23 videos a second at 60
      seconds each is 14 videos in flight at once. A GPU processes one video at
      a time, so that number is also the pool size at peak. Four are enough on
      average: the pool grows and shrinks with the queue depth.

      The queue wait is a slider, not a result: Little's law does not say what
      it will be, but it translates it into videos. Five minutes of waiting is
      69 videos in the queue at peak and six minutes from upload to clips;
      NFR-4 holds with room to spare. Push the wait to half an hour, and the
      promise is broken.

      Push the GPU factor to one, so the model looks at every frame, and the
      pool grows to seventy. One detector slider costs more than everything
      else in this design, and that is the first argument for sampling frames
      rather than running them all.
  - id: calc-storage
    title: 'Estimate: how many bytes on disk'
    narration: |-
      Now size, the reason the task is what it is.

      A five-minute phone video at 10 Mbit/s weighs 358 MB. Five thousand of
      those a day is 1.7 TB of uploads per day. That much does not pass through
      our API; that is already requirement NFR-2, and on the diagram it
      becomes the first decision.

      Clips weigh differently. A panda is in frame for a fifth of the video, a
      minute per video, three clips of twenty seconds. A clip is encoded at the
      delivery bitrate, 3 Mbit/s, not the one the phone recorded at. That is
      22 MB of clips from one video against 358 MB of original: 17 times less.
      Per day, 105 GB; per year, 37 TB.

      This gives the decision that shapes the whole storage design: **the
      original is deleted after cutting**. Push "keep the original" to thirty
      days and 51 TB is added, more than a year of clips. The user keeps the
      original on their phone; we need it only until the clips are written.

      This decision has a price too: a missed panda cannot be recovered
      without the original, and a newer, more accurate model applies only to
      new uploads. More in the detection step. The storage budget row goes
      into the table; its number comes in the retention step.

  - id: c2-queue
    title: The queue is a table
    note: 'The queue is the status, attempt and lease_until columns of the videos row'
    narration: |-
      After `complete` the video has to be processed, and the usual move is a
      broker: `video-api` publishes "video uploaded", a worker reads it. There
      is no broker here, and that is a decision, not an omission.

      The `videos` row is the task. It has the `uploaded` status, an attempt
      number, a [[lease]] deadline and a next-run time. The `clip-pipeline`
      service asks the database once a second: `SELECT … WHERE status =
      'uploaded' AND next_run_at <= now() ORDER BY … FOR UPDATE [[SKIP LOCKED]]
      LIMIT 1`, sets `processing` and a lease, and goes to work. Several
      workers take tasks from one table without getting in each other's way:
      locked rows are skipped.

      Why this beats a broker here specifically. The flow is 0.23 videos a
      second at peak; fourteen workers at one query a second is fourteen index
      lookups. The video state and the task are one row, so there is no dual
      write of database plus broker, the one that gave the scraper its outbox.
      And the queue is visible with one SQL query: how many wait, how many are
      in flight, which task is the oldest.

      A broker is for thousands of messages a second, many different
      consumers, or events that must reach several services. None of that is
      in the task: there is one consumer, and it is heavy.
    added:
      - element: pandas.pipeline
        role: 'A scheduler with the processing inside: takes the next video from the table under a lease, downloads the original, finds the panda, cuts the clips, stores them, deletes the original.'
  - id: queue-choice
    title: Table or broker
    narration: |-
      The matrix is the same requirements as in the table, against four
      options. A row is what we need, a cell is what the option gives.

      [[Kafka]] drops out first. It is a log: a consumer remembers an offset
      in a partition, not the fate of each message. A task worth a minute of
      GPU holds the whole partition, a delayed retry and a dead-letter queue
      are built by hand, and fairness between uploaders does not live in a log
      at all. And an outbox is still needed on top: the video row and the
      message are two writes.

      [[RabbitMQ]] and [[SQS]] are real work queues, and the scraper needed
      exactly that. But both have the same dual write; in RabbitMQ a message
      unacknowledged for ten minutes is a matter of timeouts and heartbeats,
      in SQS it is a visibility timeout extended by hand. Fairness between
      owners is your own code on top in both cases.

      A table in [[Postgres]] covers every row: the lease is a column, the
      retry is `next_run_at`, fairness is an `ORDER BY`, the dashboard is a
      `GROUP BY status`. It loses only on growth: hundreds of tasks a second
      on one table with `SKIP LOCKED` still work, thousands mean a broker. But
      at thousands of videos a second we would have a different problem, and
      it would not be about the queue but about petabytes.
  - id: c2-pipeline
    title: From the original to the clips
    note: 'The original is deleted after the clips commit, and never before it'
    narration: |-
      The pipeline took the task. Four actions follow, and the order matters.

      The original is downloaded from `raw-s3` once, to the worker's local
      disk. The detector samples frames from it, the cutter cuts from the same
      file: there is no reason to go to storage twice. The cut clips, [[HLS]]
      segments and a thumbnail, go to `clips-s3`, a separate store: it has a
      different lifetime and different delivery.

      Then one transaction in `clips-pg`: the clip rows and the video status
      `ready`. Until it commits, the video is still processing for the whole
      system, and the files in `clips-s3` are just files nobody refers to.

      Only after the commit does the pipeline delete the original. Delete it
      earlier and the commit fails, and the retry cuts thin air. Crash between
      the commit and the delete, and the original stays and gets picked up by
      the scheduled cleanup. So `raw-s3` holds only what is not cut yet: at
      1.7 TB a day and six minutes per video that is a few gigabytes.
    added:
      - element: pandas.media
        role: 'Clips: HLS segments and thumbnails. Kept forever, served through the CDN. Files appear here before the rows in the database and become clips only after the commit.'
  - id: c2-lease
    title: A worker died holding a video
    note: 'The lease is extended every minute of work; expired, the task goes to another'
    narration: |-
      Processing takes a minute, sometimes ten. In that time a worker can die:
      the model ran out of memory on a 4K frame, the cloud took back a spot
      machine, someone deployed a release. The video stays `processing`, and
      the dashboard does not show it, because formally it is being processed.

      So the task has a [[lease]]. Taking a video, the worker sets
      `lease_until` ten minutes ahead and extends it every minute while it
      works. The same `clip-pipeline` looks once a minute for `processing`
      rows with an expired lease and returns them to `uploaded`: attempt plus
      one, `next_run_at` after a pause that grows with every attempt. After the
      third, `failed` with the text of the last error.

      A retry must be safe. Clips are written with the key `(video_id,
      start_ms)`, files in `clips-s3` with deterministic names: half the clips
      from the previous attempt get overwritten, not doubled. That is the same
      [[idempotency]] as at intake, only inside the pipeline.

      Permanent errors, a corrupt file, not a video at all, are not worth
      retrying: those go straight to `failed`. The line runs not by error
      code but by whether a retry can give a different result.

  - id: c2-delivery
    title: 'Playback: bytes from the CDN'
    note: 'Clip bytes come from the CDN; video-api returns only the address'
    narration: |-
      `GET /clips/{id}` returns a `playback_url`, and it points not at our
      service but at the [[CDN]]. The player fetches the playlist and the
      segments from there; on a miss the CDN node pulls them from `clips-s3`
      and keeps them.

      Why a CDN, if there is already S3? Storage can serve files on its own,
      by link, and at a small traffic that is the right answer; the delivery
      calculator says so. A CDN in front of it is there for three things.

      - **Distance.** The bucket lives in one region, and a viewer from the
        other hemisphere would cross an ocean for every segment. A CDN node
        stands next to the viewer.
      - **Repeats.** A good clip is watched a hundred thousand times. Without
        a CDN that is a hundred thousand reads from the bucket; with one, the
        bucket serves a segment once per node, and the rest comes from the
        edge cache.
      - **Money.** Egress from storage to the internet is billed higher than
        egress from a CDN, and HLS is many small segments, each request to
        the bucket billed too.

      Two requirements close at once. NFR-12: 3.4 TB a day pass through
      neither `video-api` nor `api-gateway`. NFR-5: the first frame comes from
      the nearest edge, not from one region. For it to arrive within two
      seconds, segments are kept short, 2 to 4 seconds, and the clip has a
      thumbnail shown while the first segment loads.

      Clips are public, as on YouTube, so the links are plain. If product
      wants private videos, signed CDN URLs appear in this same place, and it
      is the only place that changes.

  - id: c3-pipeline
    title: 'C3: inside clip-pipeline'
    narration: |-
      We go one level down. [[C3]] shows the modules of a single service.
      `clip-pipeline` was one box on C2; inside there are four.

      - **task-poller** takes the next task under a lease, extends the lease,
        returns expired ones and deletes the original after the commit.
        Everything that belongs to the queue lives here.
      - **panda-detector** decodes the original at two frames a second, runs
        the frames through the [[detector]] in batches and merges the hits into
        ranges: with a confidence threshold, a minimum length and padding at
        the edges.
      - **clip-cutter** is [[ffmpeg]]: it cuts the ranges, encodes HLS with
        short segments, makes a thumbnail from the frame with the most
        confident panda.
      - **videos-repository** is the only module that goes to the database:
        clips and status in one transaction.

      The detector and the cutter are separate modules not only for tidiness.
      The detector needs a GPU, the cutter only a CPU, and once the pool
      grows they get separated onto different machines: an expensive graphics
      card should not wait while ffmpeg encodes.

        role: 'The whole queue: the next task under a lease, extension, returning expired ones, deleting the original after the commit.'
      - element: pandas.pipeline.detector
        role: 'Frames through the network, hits into ranges with a threshold, a minimum length and padding at the edges.'
      - element: pandas.pipeline.cutter
        role: 'ffmpeg: cuts the ranges, encodes HLS, makes thumbnails.'

  - id: data
    title: Data
    narration: |-
      Four tables, and the first of them is also the queue.

      **videos.** Who owns it, where the original is, what status it is in.
      The `attempt`, `lease_until`, `next_run_at` and `error` columns are the
      task: until the video is in a final state, the row lives in the queue.
      An index on `(status, next_run_at)` is the whole broker.

      **clips.** A range of the original in milliseconds, the detector's
      confidence, the keys of the segments and the thumbnail in `clips-s3`, a
      title and a description. And a `tags text[]` column, a copy of the tags
      for search: the index is built on it, and that is the search step.

      **tags** and **clip_tags.** The dictionary of normalised words, and who
      added which tag. The second is needed to remove your own tag and so one
      tag is not added twice. A tag is written to `clip_tags` and to
      `clips.tags` in one transaction; two copies are the price of search
      without a separate engine.

      What is not in the data: the original after cutting, and ranges without
      a clip. If the detector found something, it is a clip; if not, the video
      has the `empty` status.
  - id: api
    title: 'The contract: a receipt and addresses'
    narration: |-
      The contract as cards. Three of them are about upload, and the video
      bytes are in only one, which is not ours.

      `POST /videos` answers `201` with an id and presigned URLs for the parts.
      A `PUT` to such a URL is S3, not us: the signature limits the period and
      the object, and the client re-uploads a dropped part itself. `complete`
      is `202 Accepted`: there are no clips yet, there is a receipt and an
      address to watch.

      The move to `uploaded` is a conditional `UPDATE … WHERE status =
      'uploading'`. A repeated `complete` after a lost answer changes nothing
      and gets the same `202`: processing starts once. That is NFR-8, and the
      idempotency key here is the video id itself.

      The remaining cards are reads and tags. The `playback_url` in the answer
      points at the CDN. `DELETE /clips/{id}` closes FR-8: the files are
      deleted after it, the CDN is purged by address.
  - id: lifecycle
    title: The life of a video
    narration: |-
      A video has six states, and three of them are final.

      `uploading` → `uploaded` → `processing` → `ready`. Next to it two other
      ends: `empty`, when the detector found nothing, and `failed`, when the
      attempts ran out or the upload was never completed within a day.

      Who moves it is shown by colour. Into `uploaded`: `video-api` on
      `complete`. Into `processing` and on to `ready` or `empty`: the
      pipeline, and only after the clips commit. Back to `uploaded` and into
      `failed`: the poller by lease and attempts, not the detector itself. A
      crashed worker moves nothing; whoever notices the expired lease does it
      for it.

      `empty` is an honest state, not an error. Half the videos from a zoo
      will be of an enclosure with no panda, and the user is told so in words,
      not with an empty page.
  - id: detection
    title: 'Detection: threshold, length, errors'
    narration: |-
      The neural network is not a black box with a yes or no answer but a
      source of confidence per frame. The rules that turn it into clips are set
      by product, and they go into the requirements table.

      **Threshold.** A frame counts as a panda frame at confidence above 0.7.
      Lower means more false clips; higher means more misses. The threshold is
      tuned on a labelled sample and changed without a deploy.

      **Length and merging.** Hits are merged into a range if the pause between
      them is under two seconds; a range shorter than five seconds is dropped;
      a second is added at each edge so the panda does not walk into the frame
      on the clip's first frame.

      **Two kinds of errors.** A [[false positive]], a clip with a raccoon, is
      fixed by the uploader through FR-8, and that is cheap. A miss, a panda
      with no clip, cannot be fixed at all without the original: we deleted
      it. That is the price of the storage decision, and it has to be named to
      product explicitly. If misses are expensive, originals are kept for a
      day or two, at 1.7 TB a day, and the uploader can ask for another pass.

      **A new model.** Applies to new uploads. The archive cannot be
      reprocessed for the same reason.
  - id: fairness
    title: A noisy neighbour on the GPUs
    narration: |-
      Uploader isolation is written down as one row. The simulator on the
      right shows what it means.

      Three users: a tourist and a school trip with a video every two seconds,
      and a zoo that uploaded its archive, four hundred videos at once. A pool
      of eight GPUs at four seconds per video works off two videos a second.
      Switch on the shared queue: the zoo occupies the pool for three minutes,
      and the tourist has not a single clip ready in that time. The system is
      right all along: it takes tasks in order of arrival.

      Switch to "equal share". The pool is the same; only the rule for the
      next task changed: whoever has the fewest in flight goes next. The
      tourist gets clips in seconds, the zoo gets a third of the pool.

      In a table this is one `ORDER BY` in the poller's query: owners with
      fewer in flight first, then by upload time. What a broker would need a
      per-owner queue and a scheduler of your own for is one line of SQL
      here. Weights, more for a paying zoo, are the same line with a
      coefficient.
  - id: search
    title: Where search lives
    narration: |-
      Tag search is not full-text search. It is an exact match of words from
      a dictionary, and it needs no separate engine.

      A tag is normalised on the way in: lower case, trimmed spaces, two to
      thirty-two characters. The normalised words sit in `clips.tags`, with a
      [[GIN]] index built on it. The query "clips with tags `panda` and
      `cub`" is `WHERE tags @> ARRAY['panda','cub']`, one pass over the index
      and a sort by time. At 28 reads a second that is milliseconds.

      Freshness comes for free: the tag is written to `clips.tags` in the same
      transaction as `clip_tags`, and search sees it right after the commit.
      NFR-6 asked for five seconds; we got zero.

      Suggestions while typing are a separate query to the `tags` dictionary
      by prefix, with an index for `LIKE 'pan%'`.

      When [[OpenSearch]] or the like becomes needed: full-text search over
      descriptions with typo tolerance, ranking by popularity, thousands of
      searches a second. The delivery calculator shows thousands are far
      away. Until then an engine is an extra component with its own sync and
      its own lag, which is exactly what would break NFR-6.
  - id: retention
    title: What to keep, and for how long
    narration: |-
      Three classes of data with different fates.

      **Originals.** Deleted after the clips commit, and a scheduled cleanup
      picks up what crashed workers and abandoned uploads left behind: a day,
      and the object is gone. `raw-s3` permanently holds a few gigabytes,
      although 1.7 TB a day pass through it.

      **Clips.** Kept while the video exists: 37 TB a year at the current
      flow. That is a budget, not a storage problem; after a year or two old
      clips with no views go to a cold storage tier, and that is a storage
      rule, not our code. Deleting a video deletes its clips with the files
      and purges the CDN by address.

      **Rows in the database.** Millions of clips a year is a table Postgres
      barely notices. `videos` needs attention of its own: the status changes
      several times in a row's life, and dead versions pile up. Autovacuum
      copes, but it has to be watched, because the queue runs on this same
      table.

      The number for NFR-10 goes into the table: the original after the
      commit, clips about 37 TB a year.
  - id: observability
    title: What to measure
    narration: |-
      There are many metrics; five are enough for decisions.

      - **The age of the oldest task in `uploaded`.** More important than the
        queue length: the pool grows by it, and it shows the pool is not
        keeping up before NFR-4 breaks.
      - **Time from `complete` to `ready`, p95.** Maps directly to the SLO.
      - **Failures by error class.** "Out of memory", "cannot decode", "model
        did not answer" are three different incidents, and they cannot be
        mixed into one failure rate.
      - **The share of `empty`.** Doubled in a day means the threshold drifted
        or the model degraded, and users have not complained yet.
      - **CDN hit ratio and egress from `clips-s3`.** If storage egress grows
        with views, the CDN is not caching, and the storage bill will say so
        later than the metric.

      And stage with attempts on the video page: the on-call engineer should
      not need the database to see "attempt 2 of 3, crashed on frame 1,840".
  - id: tradeoffs
    title: What it cost
    narration: |-
      The system keeps all four promises from the brief. Here is what was
      given up for that.

      **There is no original.** A missed panda cannot be recovered, a new
      model will not reprocess the archive, manual editing is impossible. This
      is a decision for the sake of storage, and it should be written down so
      nobody looks for the originals in a year.

      **The queue lives in the database.** One database is one failure
      domain: Postgres down means intake, processing and the gallery are down.
      The queue table needs attention to vacuum, and growth to thousands of
      tasks a second will require a broker and an outbox.

      **One service for writes and reads.** They scale together. With views
      up two orders of magnitude, the read side will have to be split out with
      a cache in front of it.

      **The CDN is the main line on the bill.** The bytes left our services,
      but they did not disappear: 102 TB a month cost money, and product pays
      for them.

      **Product quality is bounded by the model.** The threshold and merging
      rules can be tuned, but "the wrong panda" is solved only by a better
      model, and it applies to new videos.

      The decision gets revisited if private videos appear (signed CDN URLs),
      live streams (different processing), or a flow of hundreds of videos a
      second (a broker, and the pipeline split into GPU and CPU pools).

    options:

      - As soon as the detector has found all the ranges
      - Never, the original is kept with the clips
    answer: 0
    explanation: >-
      Deleting before the commit leaves a retry without its source. Deleting
      after is safe: if the worker crashes between the commit and the delete,

    options:

    explanation: >-

The second full-task write-up, and a different task. In the scraping jobs
service the queue decided everything: 28 jobs a second, the peak, the pool,
priorities. Here the flow is a fraction of a video a second, and the queue is
not a problem at all.

The problem is the unit of work. One video is hundreds of megabytes on the
way in, a minute of GPU inside, and clips watched thousands of times. So the
write-up, following the same order of understand, ask, write down, estimate,
draw, arrives at different decisions: bytes flow past the API, the queue
lives in a table, the original is deleted, and a <span data-term="CDN">CDN</span>
does the serving.

The order is the same:

1. **Understand what is asked.** Retell the task as a set of promises the
   system makes.
2. **Ask.** Find out everything the brief lacks that the solution needs.
3. **Write down the requirements.** Functional: what the system can do.
   Non-functional: with what properties.
4. **Estimate.** GPUs, bytes on disk, bytes in delivery.
5. **Draw.** <span data-term="C1">C1</span>, <span data-term="C2">C2</span> and below, justifying
   every decision with numbers.
