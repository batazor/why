description: >-
  One equality for three questions: how many workers are needed, why the queue
  is growing, and how long a message waits in it.
problem: >-
  Pool size gets guessed from the user count, the queue threshold gets set at
  "how much is too much", and waiting time is never computed at all. All three
  numbers are tied by one equality, and what goes into it is already measured.

terms:
  λ: >-
    The arrival flow: how many units of work enter the system per second. Lambda
    in the notation of Little's law.
  W: >-
    The time one unit of work spends inside the system: waiting plus service.
  L: >-
    How many units of work are inside the system at once. The number the law is
    computed for in the first place.
  stable system: >-
    A system where, over a long enough window, as much leaves as enters. Its

steps:
  - id: problem
    title: How many are in there
    narration: |-
      "How many workers do we need" is usually settled by a guess: take the user
      count, divide it by something, round up. "Why is the queue growing" gets
      closed with a line about load. And "how long does a message wait in the
      queue" tends not to be asked at all — it is not clear where to look.

      All three come down to one number, and it does not have to be guessed. John
      Little proved this in 1961, in a five-page paper.
  - id: law
    title: 'L = λ × W'
    narration: |-
      Take anything work enters and leaves: a message queue, a worker pool, a
      supermarket till, an emergency room. How many units of work are inside is
      the arrival flow multiplied by the time one unit spends there.

      [[L]] = [[λ]] × [[W]]. Ten requests a second, each living inside for thirty
      seconds — three hundred are always inside. The dimensions work out on their
      own: items per second times seconds gives items.

      The formula was in use before Little, but as a rule of thumb. He proved it,
      and along the way showed what the proof needs: stationary processes and
      finite means for all three quantities. Nothing about how arrivals are
      distributed, in what order things are served, or how many servers there are.

      That is what makes it useful. Queueing theory needs to know the arrival
      distribution — Poisson, deterministic, something else; without it there is
      nothing to compute. Little does not care. Steady arrivals, bursts of a
      thousand, service in order, from the back or by priority — the equality
      holds.
  - id: calc
    title: Three quantities, move any of them
    narration: |-
      The calculator is on the right. Move the flow and the number inside moves.
      Move the time and it moves the same way. The two factors are equals.

      Which gives the conclusion the formula is remembered for: halving service
      time is the same thing as halving the load. A job that takes ten seconds
      instead of twenty means half as many inside and half the pool. Same users,
      same requests.

      It works the other way round too, and that direction is usually noticed
      first. A third-party API got three times slower and the hardware bill
      tripled. Not one of our own numbers changed.
  - id: split
    title: The queue is a difference
    narration: |-
      The box can be anything with an entrance and an exit. So the law applies to
      a part of the system as well — and that is where it starts answering the
      questions worth knowing it for.

      Apply it twice. To the whole system: λ × W, everything inside. To service:
      λ × S, where S is service time without the waiting. The difference is the
      queue.

      Ten a second, thirty seconds inside, ten of them service. Three hundred
      inside, a hundred being served, two hundred waiting. Those two hundred are
      not the output of a simulation; the equality does not balance otherwise.

      What matters next is not mixing up which of the two numbers the pool is
      sized by. A waiting unit sits in the queue and holds no worker, so the
      figure to use is λ × S. Sizing by λ × W buys three times more machines than
      the work needs.

      Brian Goetz writes a related form of this in Java Concurrency in Practice:
      threads = cores × target utilisation × (1 + wait time / service time). The
      ratio in brackets is the same wait-to-service split, and it is why a pool
      needs more threads than the machine has cores.
  - id: inverse
    title: 'The other direction: how long the wait is'
    narration: |-
      The same equality solved for W: W = L / λ.

      This is how queue charts are read. "Forty thousand messages" says nothing on
      its own — it can be a working state or an outage. Divide by the drain rate:
      two hundred a second means two hundred seconds. The threshold "depth above
      forty thousand" actually reads "a message waits more than three minutes".

      Hence the advice usually handed over without explanation: alert on the age
      of the oldest message, not on depth. Age is W, the thing promised to the
      user. Depth without λ means nothing: a thousand messages drained at ten
      thousand a second is a tenth of a second, while a hundred messages drained
      at one a second is over a minute and a half.
  - id: stable
    title: The one condition
    narration: |-
      There is exactly one condition: the system has to be
      [[stable system|stable]]. Over a long enough window, as much leaves it as
      enters. Little states it more strictly — stationary processes and finite L,
      λ and W — but this is what gets checked in practice.

      When the flow is larger than the system manages to serve, there is no
      stability. The queue grows, the time inside grows with it, and no average W
      exists: it goes up every minute. The equality does not break, it honestly
      reports that L is growing. There is just nothing to predict with it, and
      what has to be computed is not W but the gap between flow and capacity.

      Capacity comes from the same two numbers: servers divided by service time.
      A hundred servers at ten seconds each is exactly ten units a second. If the
      flow is also ten, utilisation is a hundred per cent. That is not "it worked
      out perfectly" but the state where any spike turns into a queue that will
      not drain again.

      And about the window. It has to be long relative to W. For a job a minute
      long the law answers about half an hour, not about what happened in one
      particular minute.
  - id: traps
    title: What it does not promise
    narration: |-
      The law is exact, which is exactly why it is easy to stretch over things it
      says nothing about.

      Its quantities are averages. Three hundred inside can mean a steady three
      hundred, or zero for half a day and three thousand at the evening peak. A
      pool sized by the average will not hold at peak; that is where the separate
      unevenness multiplier in estimates comes from.

      It says nothing about spread either. An average wait of twenty seconds sits
      comfortably with one per cent of requests waiting ten minutes. Which is why
      promises to users are written as percentiles and never as averages.

      And it does not answer what happens when load grows. A capacity of ten units
      a second does not mean nine is fine. The closer utilisation gets to one, the
      longer the queue, and it grows with acceleration rather than linearly. By
      how much is queueing theory, and there distributions are unavoidable. Little
      tells you how many are inside at a given W. What W becomes as λ grows, he
      does not.
  - id: answer
    title: What to do with it
    narration: |-
      Three substitutions into one equality:

      - how many servers — λ × S divided by what one of them holds;
      - how many are queueing — λ × W minus λ × S;
      - how long one unit waits — L divided by λ.

      Outside engineering the same law is spoken in other words. Kanban writes it
      as "cycle time = WIP / throughput", and work-in-progress limits are an
      attempt to hold L down so that W does not run away. Different box, same
      formula.

      None of it starts with the formula. It starts with the question of what the
      box is here, what enters it, and how long a unit stays inside. The
      multiplication comes after. The [scraping jobs walkthrough](../scraping-jobs/)
      sizes its pool this way — there you can watch one multiplication turn into
      an architecture decision.

    explanation: >-
      L = λ × W. The flow did not change, the time inside doubled, so the number
      inside doubled. How jobs arrive is something the law does not care about.

    explanation: >-
      W = L / λ: 20,000 / 1,000 = 20 seconds. An average: an unlucky message gets
      more than that, and how much more the law does not say.

      - One hundred

    answer: 2
    explanation: >-
      The two hundred waiting sit in the queue and hold no server. The pool is
      sized by λ × S, that is, by service.
