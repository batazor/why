steps:
  - id: problem
    title: How many are in there
    narration: >-
      "How many workers do we need?" — "Well, fifty thousand users, let's say
      twenty machines." "Why is the queue growing?" — "Load went up." "How long
      does a message wait?" — that one usually gets no answer at all, because
      nobody knows where to look. Three conversations, three guesses. It is one
      question, and its answer is not guessed: it follows from two numbers both
      people already have at that moment.
  - id: law
    title: 'L = λ × W'
    narration: |-
      Take any system that things enter and leave: a message queue, a worker
      pool, a supermarket till, an emergency room. How many units of work are
      inside it at once is the arrival flow multiplied by the time one unit
      spends inside.

      [[L]] = [[λ]] × [[W]]. Ten requests a second, each living inside for thirty
      seconds — three hundred are always inside. The units work out on their own:
      items per second multiplied by seconds gives items.

      What is remarkable about the law is not what it requires but what it does
      **not**. It does not care how units arrive: in a steady stream, Poisson, in
      bursts of a thousand. It does not care in what order they are served: in
      turn, from the back, by priority, at random. It does not care how many
      servers there are or whether they are equally fast. There is no
      distribution to plug in.

      That is why it works where ordinary queueing theory already fails: queueing
      theory needs to know how arrivals are distributed, and that data is not
      always there. The equality is not an approximation and not a model. Under
      one condition, covered in the step on stability, it holds exactly.
  - id: calc
    title: Three quantities, move any of them
    narration: |-
      The calculator is on the right. Move "arrives per second" and the number
      inside moves with it. Move "stays inside" and it moves exactly the same
      way. The law is symmetric: doubling the flow and doubling the time give one
      and the same result.

      From this comes the consequence it is most often recalled for: **making
      service faster is the same thing as reducing load.** If a job takes ten
      seconds instead of twenty, half as many are inside, and the pool halves —
      at the same flow and the same users. Optimising one request and switching
      off half the clients do the same thing to pool size.

      And the other way round: a slow third-party dependency raises our hardware
      bill without touching a single number of ours. Same flow, same users, three
      times as many inside, because the target started answering three times
      slower.
  - id: split
    title: The queue is a difference
    narration: |-
      The law applies not only to the system as a whole. It holds for any part of
      it that has an entrance and an exit — and this is where it starts answering
      the questions worth knowing it for.

      Apply it twice. To the whole system: λ × W, everything inside. To service
      alone: λ × S, where S is the service time without waiting. The first number
      is every unit inside, the second is the ones actually being worked on. The
      difference between them is the queue.

      Ten a second, thirty seconds inside, ten of them service. Three hundred
      inside, a hundred being served, two hundred waiting. Not because we decided
      so — because otherwise the equality does not hold.

      That gives the answer about the pool. **Size it by service, not by
      everything inside.** A waiting unit sits in the queue and holds no
      resource: a hundred servers are enough for three hundred units inside.
      Sizing the pool by λ × W means buying three times more machines than needed
      and letting them idle.
  - id: inverse
    title: 'The other direction: how long the wait is'
    narration: |-
      The same equality solved for time: W = L / λ. How many units are inside,
      divided by the flow, is how long a unit spends inside.

      This is how monitoring is read. "Forty thousand messages in the queue"
      means nothing by itself: it can be a normal working state or an outage.
      Divide it by the drain rate. Two hundred a second — forty thousand take two
      hundred seconds. Now it is clear: the threshold "depth above forty
      thousand" is really the threshold "a message waits more than three
      minutes".

      Hence a rule often stated without explanation: **alert on the age of the
      oldest item, not on queue depth.** Age is W, the very thing promised to the
      user. Depth is L, a number that means nothing without λ: a thousand
      messages drained at ten thousand a second is harmless, a hundred drained at
      one a second is already an outage.
  - id: stable
    title: The one condition
    narration: |-
      The law has one condition: the system must be [[stable|stable system]].
      Over a long enough window, as much leaves it as enters.

      If the flow exceeds what the system manages to serve, there is no
      stability. The queue grows, the time inside grows with it, and no "average
      W" exists — it increases every minute. The law does not break here: it
      still holds and honestly reports that L is growing. There is simply nothing
      to predict with it. What has to be computed in that situation is not W but
      the gap between flow and capacity — and then fixed.

      Capacity comes out of the same numbers: servers divided by service time. A
      hundred servers at ten seconds each is exactly ten units a second. At a flow
      of ten a second utilisation is a hundred per cent, and that is not "it
      worked out perfectly" but the state in which any unevenness turns into a
      growing queue.

      The second thing easy to forget: the window has to be long compared with W.
      For a job a minute long the law answers about tens of minutes, not about
      what happens inside one of them.
  - id: traps
    title: What it does not promise
    narration: |-
      The law is exact, but it answers exactly what it was asked. Three things it
      does not say, and all three get attributed to it regularly.

      **It is not about peaks.** All three quantities are averages. Three hundred
      inside on average can mean a steady three hundred, or zero one moment and
      three thousand the next. A pool sized by the average will not hold at peak,
      which is where the separate unevenness multiplier in estimates comes from.

      **It is not about spread.** The law does not say how long the unluckiest
      unit waits. An average wait of twenty seconds is compatible with one per
      cent waiting ten minutes. That is exactly why promises to users are written
      in percentiles rather than averages.

      **It is not about what happens as load grows.** A capacity of ten units a
      second does not imply that nine is fine. The closer utilisation gets to one,
      the longer the queue, and it grows sharply rather than linearly. How
      sharply is queueing theory, and there distributions are unavoidable.
      Little's law tells you how many are inside at a given W; it does not tell
      you what W becomes as λ grows.
  - id: answer
    title: What to do with it
    narration: |-
      One question in three shapes, and every answer is a substitution into the
      equality.

      - **How many servers.** λ × S ÷ what one of them holds. Sized by service,
        not by everything inside.
      - **How many are queueing.** λ × W − λ × S. Waiting is the difference
        between two applications of one law.
      - **How long one unit waits.** L ÷ λ. This is how queue depth in monitoring
        turns into a number worth showing to the product.

      Start not with the formula but with the question: what is the box here,
      what enters it, and how long does a unit stay inside. The multiplication
      comes after.
