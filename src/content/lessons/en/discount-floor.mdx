problem: >-
  Two promotions, each approved and each reasonable, land on the same item and
  sell it below what you paid for it. No line of code was wrong — that pair was
  simply never added up.

steps:
  - id: one
    title: One promo, and you can see the rule
    narration: >-
      A single mechanic is easy to reason about: list price, one percentage off,
      and a human can check that the result stays above what the item cost us.
      The rule is never written down anywhere, because with one promo it does not
      need to be.
    note: One promo. The rule is checked by eye.
  - id: stack
    title: The second one arrives three weeks later
    narration: >-
      Both promos are approved, both are tested, and each is correct. What nobody
      tested is the pair — and with thirty active promotions there are 435 pairs
      and four thousand triples, changing every week without a single deploy. This
      one sells a 32.00 item for 10.00.
    note: Each promo is fine. The pair is not.
  - id: clamp
    title: The fix everyone reaches for first
    narration: >-
      Clamp the result at cost. The customer now pays 32.00 and the incident is
      closed. Look at the three numbers though: the customer paid 32.00, the
      discount actually given was 18.00, and the report still says 40.00, because
      it asks the promo engine rather than the receipt. The money stopped leaking
      and the books started lying.
    note: The number is fixed. The report is not.
  - id: rule
    title: Write the rule down as a claim
    narration: >-
      Not a test on a known pair - a claim about every item and every set of
      promos. And the first thing that claim buys you is bad news: Lean does not
      merely fail to prove it, it proves the opposite, and the counterexample
      becomes part of the build. You now know the model is wrong rather than
      suspecting it.
    note: Not unproven — disproven, with the pair attached.
  - id: budget
    title: Make the promos share a budget
    narration: >-
      The margin is 18.00, and that is the whole pool the promos have. Each one
      takes what it asks for or what is left, whichever is smaller. Normal cases
      are untouched — a lone 20% still gives 40.00 — and the catastrophic set now
      lands exactly on the floor. The proof is one line, because the rule is no
      longer something we check: it is something the model cannot break.
    note: Discounts spend margin. There is no more to spend.
  - id: evolve
    title: The next mechanic cannot sneak in
    narration: >-
      Marketing asks for cashback. The new mechanic does not silently default to
      something - it simply does not compile until someone says what it takes out
      of the pool. That is the difference the whole lesson is about: the question
      is asked at the moment the mechanic is added, not during the incident.
    note: A new mechanic must declare its share.

      The final price is cost plus whatever is left of the pool, so it is at least

Discount engines fail in a particular way. Not because a mechanic is wrong, but
because mechanics multiply and nobody owns their combinations. Marketing adds
promos through an admin panel, without a deploy and without a review, and the
number of pairs grows quadratically while the number of tests stays flat.

The damage is quiet: you do not get an exception, you get a sale. It is noticed
when someone looks at margin by SKU at the end of the month, or when a deal site
finds the combination first and posts it.

## Where this shows up

Anywhere rules compose without an owner: promotions, shipping-cost rules, tariff
plans, loyalty tiers, commission schedules for marketplaces.

## What this is not

This is not an argument that you cannot ship pricing without a proof assistant.
It is an argument about what kind of statement you need. "This pair is fine" is a
test, and there will never be enough of them. "No set of promos can take the price
below cost" is a different kind of sentence, and it is the one the business
actually meant.

compiler output.
