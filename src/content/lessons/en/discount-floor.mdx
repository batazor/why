      and that is what separates this from a test, which speaks about one case.
      So far it is only a statement: neither proved nor disproved.
    note: The rule is written. Nothing checked yet.
  - id: refute
    title: Assume it holds
    narration: >-
      The pair already dropped the price to 10.00, but so far that is an
      observation: the compiler knows nothing about it and will not bring it up
      the next time someone edits the engine. To make it know, you state the
      negation of the rule as a theorem and prove it. In Lean `¬P` is shorthand
      for `P → False`, so the job is to derive a contradiction from the
      assumption. That is what `intro` does — it takes the premise and gives it a
      name. Now `rule` is the assumption "the rule holds", and what is left to
      prove is `False`.
    note: We assumed the rule holds.
  - id: instantiate
    title: Apply it to one item
    narration: >-
      `rule` speaks about every item and every set of promos at once. Apply it to
      one — these sneakers and that very pair. Out comes `bad`: 3200 ≤ 1000. The
      numbers plainly do not add up, and that is the point — `bad` rests on the
      assumption made a step earlier, and it falls together with it.
    note: Applied to a single pair.
  - id: contradict
    title: Reach a contradiction
    narration: >-
      Take the line apart. `bad` is "3200 ≤ 1000", obtained from the assumption.
      `(by decide)` proves the opposite: Lean simply evaluates the same
      inequality and gets "false". `absurd` takes a claim together with its
      negation and produces anything at all — from a contradiction everything
      follows; what is needed from it here is `False`. `exact` hands that `False`
      to the goal, and the theorem is closed. So the assumption fails: the rule
      does not hold. The counterexample stays in the file, and the compiler
      re-runs it on every build.
    note: Disproven, with the pair attached.

    title: The pool the discounts are paid from
    narration: >-
      Everything the promos may spend is the margin: the gap between the list
      price and cost. On these sneakers that is 18.00. Until now promos counted
      off the price and knew nothing about cost; now they share a wallet.
    note: The discount pool is the margin, 18.00.
  - id: spend
    title: You cannot draw on an empty wallet
    narration: >-
      `spend` walks the list and for each promo subtracts the `min` of two
      numbers: what it asks for and what is left in the pool. The pool never goes
      negative — a greedy promo simply takes the remainder and nothing beyond it.
    note: A promo takes its cut or the remainder, whichever is smaller.
  - id: proof
    title: A one-line proof
    narration: >-
      The total is cost plus whatever is left of the pool, and what is left is a
      natural number, never negative. So the total is never below cost, and that
      is visible from the shape of the expression: `Nat.le_add_right` says "a
      number is no greater than itself plus something". Nothing left to check — a
      lone 20% still pays 40.00, three greedy promos land on 32.00.
    note: Total is cost plus a non-negative remainder.
  - id: evolve
    title: The next mechanic
    narration: >-
      Marketing asks for cashback. The file stops compiling until someone says
      what this mechanic takes off the price. The proof needs no rewrite: it
      never looks inside a mechanic.

    title: Declare its share
    narration: >-

      And `checkout_never_below_cost` is unchanged word for word: the proof never
      looks inside `cut`, so a new mechanic does not concern it.
    note: Mechanic declared. The proof is unchanged.

A test is a sentence about one case. <span class="claim">"This pair of promos is
fine"</span> gets checked and passes. More promos keep arriving and the pairs
between them grow quadratically. Nobody writes that many tests.

Nothing crashes. The order goes through the normal path and shows up in
monitoring as a successful sale. Somebody finds it at month end, looking at
<span data-term="margin">margin</span> per <span data-term="SKU">SKU</span>. Or
earlier, if an employee stumbles onto the combination by accident.

**Lean** lets you write a different sentence: <span class="claim">"no set of
promos takes the price below cost"</span>. That one covers every case at once,
and it has no green-tests state — it is either proved or refuted by a specific
pair.

The compiler catches one more thing. If we ever want to add a new kind of
discount — cashback, say — the file stops compiling. The function that computes
the size of a discount matches on the promo type case by case, the new case has
no branch, and Lean names the missing one out loud. Until that branch is written,
until somebody decides what cashback takes off the price, there is no build. The
rule itself is never at risk: the proof never looks inside a mechanic, so cashback
cannot push the total below cost whatever it computes.

## Where else rules add up

Shipping rules, tariff plans, loyalty tiers, marketplace commission schedules.
Anywhere rules compose and nobody owns the sum.

## Where the line is

You can ship pricing without a <span data-term="proof assistant">proof
assistant</span>. The question is what kind of sentence you need.
<span class="claim">"This pair is fine"</span> is a test, and there will never be
enough of them. <span class="claim">"No set of promos takes the price below
cost"</span> is what the business asked for in the first place.

The `Pricing.lean` file from this lesson compiles as a whole: Lean 4.34, no
errors. The numbers in the output are what the compiler printed.
